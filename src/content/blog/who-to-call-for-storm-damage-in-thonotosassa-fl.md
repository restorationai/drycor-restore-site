**TL;DR:** For storm damage in Thonotosassa, FL, the order matters: call 911 or the utility first for downed lines or a gas smell, get a tarp or board-up on any open roof or broken window within hours to stop further damage, start water extraction within 24-48 hours to head off mold, and call your insurance company after mitigation begins, not before. If a storm just rolled through Thonotosassa and you've got a tree limb through the roof, water coming in around a window, or a line down in the yard, you don't have time to figure out which number to dial first. The order you make these calls in actually changes how fast the damage stops and how smoothly your insurance claim goes. Here's the decision tree, in the sequence it needs to happen.

## Is there a downed power line or a gas smell? Call 911 and the utility first.

A downed line or a gas odor is a life-safety call, not a restoration call: dial 911 immediately, then contact the utility serving your address (Tampa Electric, known locally as TECO, serves most of Thonotosassa) to report the outage or hazard.

Stay at least 30 feet back from any downed line and assume every line is live, even if it looks dead or the power is out at your house. If you smell gas, don't flip any switches, light anything, or start a generator near the house; get everyone outside and call from a safe distance. Hillsborough County's emergency management office also tracks storm-related hazards countywide and is worth a call if you're unsure which agency handles a specific hazard on your street.

Once the line is confirmed down, reported, or the hazard is cleared, move to the next step: what's actually open on your house.

## Is the roof open or a window broken? Get it covered within hours, not days.

A roof open to the sky or a broken window needs emergency tarping or board-up the same day, because every additional hour of exposure adds more water, more mold risk, and more repair cost. This is also the step most homeowners skip or delay, and it's the one insurers scrutinize hardest.

A licensed restoration crew carries tarps, plywood, fasteners, and the ladder safety gear to do this correctly on a storm-damaged roof, which is different from a DIY tarp job that blows off in the next gust. [DRYCOR RESTORE's board-up and tarping crew](/services/emergency-board-up-tarping/) secures openings, photographs the damage before and after, and documents the work for the claim file. That documentation matters because most homeowners policies require you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage; skip the tarp and an adjuster can argue later damage wasn't storm-caused.

If water already got past the opening before you could seal it, you're now on the clock for extraction.

## Is water already inside? Extraction needs to start within 24-48 hours.

Standing or trapped water inside the house needs professional extraction within 24 to 48 hours, because wet drywall and framing can start growing mold in that same window under the Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification's guidance. Waiting a few extra days to "see if it dries on its own" is the single most common mistake that turns a wind-damage claim into a mold claim.

Whether the water came from a roof breach, a blown-in window, or wind-driven rain that worked past the flashing, it needs to be pulled out with extraction equipment and the structure dried to a measured standard, not just mopped and fans turned on. [Emergency water removal](/services/emergency-water-removal/) crews use truck-mounted extractors and commercial dehumidifiers, and dry the structure to the moisture readings the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) calls for, with daily readings logged for the insurance file. A full walk-through of what that process looks like day by day is covered in our [hurricane storm damage repair guide](/blog/hurricane-storm-damage-repair-thonotosassa-fl/).

Once the bleeding has stopped, meaning the hazard is handled, the opening is covered, and water is coming out, it's time to call the insurance company.

## When do I call my insurance company, and what do I tell them?

Call your insurer after mitigation has started, with photos and a description of what's been done, rather than waiting for their inspection before you tarp or extract. Most policies require you to prevent further damage; acting first and documenting it protects the claim instead of jeopardizing it.

When you call, have the date and time of the storm, photos of the damage before any tarping, and the name of the company doing the mitigation work ready to give the adjuster. Keep every receipt and every before-and-after photo. If the damage involves wind-driven rain that entered through a storm-created opening, that's typically treated differently under a standard homeowners policy than rising floodwater or storm surge, which generally require a separate flood policy; the [Insurance Information Institute's guide to disaster coverage](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance) breaks down that distinction, and [FloodSmart.gov](https://www.floodsmart.gov/) covers how flood insurance works separately from a homeowners policy. Check your specific policy language, since coverage varies by insurer.

The first call is a short conversation: your address, what's damaged, whether water is actively entering, and whether anyone is in immediate danger.
