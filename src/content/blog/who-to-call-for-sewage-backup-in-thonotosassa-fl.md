## Is the backup coming from the public sewer main, your private line, or a septic tank?

Where the backup originates decides who to call first. If multiple fixtures across the lowest level of the house are backing up at once, or a neighbor on the same street mentions the same problem, the blockage is likely in the public sewer main and the local water and sewer utility needs to know. If only your home is affected and nothing is happening next door, the problem is almost always in your own private lateral line or, in parts of Hillsborough County that aren't on municipal sewer, your septic system.

A private lateral line clog (tree roots, grease buildup, a collapsed section of old pipe) calls for a plumber or a licensed septic contractor to clear the blockage first. A municipal main backup is reported to the county or city water utility, since it isn't a problem a private contractor can fix at the source. Either way, if sewage has already reached your floor, cleanup starts regardless of which pipe failed.

## Should I call a plumber or a cleanup crew first?

If water or sewage is still actively flowing into the house, call a plumber first to stop the source; if it has already stopped and sewage is sitting on your floor, cleanup needs to start immediately and can run alongside the plumbing repair. The two jobs don't have to wait on each other. A crew that offers [emergency plumbing](/services/emergency-plumbing/) and [sewage cleanup](/services/sewage-cleanup/) under one roof can diagnose the clog, clear it, and start extraction the same visit.

Waiting makes the second job worse. Sewage sitting on subfloor, carpet, or drywall for more than a few hours lets contamination soak deeper into porous materials, which usually means more has to be removed rather than dried in place.

## Is it safe to stay in a house with sewage backup?

No. Any backup from a drain, toilet, or floor drain beyond the trap is classified as Category 3 water, also called black water, under the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), and it carries bacteria, viruses, and other pathogens. Keep children, pets, and anyone with a compromised immune system away from the affected area until a professional has removed contaminated materials and treated the space.

Don't try to mop or wet-vac sewage water yourself with household equipment. A household shop vac isn't built to handle it safely and can aerosolize contaminated water into the air you're breathing.

## My yard is flooded too. Is it safe to pump the septic tank right now?

No, not while the drain field or surrounding yard is still saturated. The Florida Department of Health advises against pumping a septic tank during or immediately after flooding, because a pumped-out tank can float or shift in saturated soil, and the drain field won't be able to accept effluent until the ground drains. Wait until the yard visibly dries before scheduling a pump-out, and in the meantime keep water use in the house to a minimum.

## Does homeowners insurance cover sewage backup?

Most standard homeowners policies do not cover sewer or sump pump backups unless you've added a water backup endorsement. Without that endorsement, a backup from a city main, a private lateral, or a failed sump pump is typically excluded, even though sudden water damage from a burst pipe inside the home usually is covered. Check your [declarations page](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance) for a water backup or sewer backup rider before you assume either way, and keep photos plus receipts from the moment you discover the problem.

## What happens when you call for sewage backup cleanup?

The first call is a short conversation: what fixtures are backing up, how long it's been happening, and whether water is standing on the floor. That tells the crew what equipment to bring and whether a plumber needs to go first.

On arrival, the technician identifies the source, confirms the water category, and starts extraction of standing sewage with truck-mounted equipment rather than household tools. Contaminated porous materials like carpet pad, baseboard, and sometimes drywall are removed rather than dried in place, because Category 3 water usually can't be safely cleaned out of those materials. The remaining structure is treated with an antimicrobial, then dried and monitored with moisture meters until readings return to normal. A written scope of work documents everything removed, which matters for the insurance claim regardless of what the policy covers.
