## Is anyone in danger right now?

Before any phone call, get everyone out of the rooms directly under or near the damage and stay out until a professional confirms the structure is stable. A tree through a roof can leave rafters cracked or a ceiling holding weight it was never built for, and that is not something to test by walking underneath it.

Look outside before you walk the yard. If any part of the tree is touching a power line or a line is down anywhere nearby, stay back and call your utility provider, not 911 for a non-emergency, and not a tree service. Downed lines stay live even when they look dead. If you smell gas, leave the house and call the gas company from outside.

Once the scene is confirmed safe, take photos of the tree, the hole, and any water coming in before you touch anything. Your insurer and your contractor will both want that documentation.

## Who do I actually call, and in what order?

Call your homeowners insurer first to open the claim, then a tree removal company to get the trunk and limbs off the structure, then a storm damage restoration company to close up the hole and handle whatever water got inside. Each of those three does a different job, and none of them substitutes for the others.

Your insurer assigns a claim number and, for many Florida carriers, that claim number is what a restoration crew needs on file before starting emergency work under your policy. Call them early, even from the yard.

Tree removal is its own trade. A storm restoration crew, including DRYCOR RESTORE, does not cut down or haul away trees. That work takes a crane, a bucket truck, chainsaw crews insured for that specific hazard, and often a permit depending on the size of the tree and whether it is blocking a right-of-way. Call an arborist or tree removal company for that part, and ask if they coordinate with restoration crews regularly since that handoff happens often after Florida storms.

Once the tree is off, the race is against water, not the tree. Rain through an open roof can soak insulation, drywall, and framing within hours, and mold can begin growing on wet material in as little as 24 to 48 hours according to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home). That is the restoration company's job: emergency tarping or board-up, water extraction, and drying the structure before it becomes a second problem on top of the hole in the roof.

## What happens when you call a restoration crew?

A restoration crew's first job on the phone is to get your address, the claim number if you have it, and a description of where the opening is, then dispatch a crew for emergency tarping and water mitigation. DRYCOR RESTORE answers calls 24/7 and documents the loss with photos and moisture readings before any work starts, which becomes part of your insurance file.

On arrival, the crew tarps or boards the opening first to stop ongoing water intrusion, since every additional hour of rain through a hole makes the drying job bigger. They will walk the attic and the rooms below with a moisture meter to map how far the water traveled, because a tree strike rarely stays confined to the room directly under the hole.

If the water reached drywall, insulation, or flooring, extraction and structural drying follow the same [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) used on any water loss, with air movers and dehumidifiers run until moisture readings confirm the materials are dry, not just dry to the touch. ## Does homeowners insurance cover a tree falling on the house?

Most standard homeowners policies cover the structural damage when a tree falls on a covered building, because wind and falling objects are typically named covered perils, but tree removal coverage often has its own smaller limit. According to the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance), the roof, drywall, and framing repair is usually treated under dwelling coverage, while removing the tree itself may be capped at a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars depending on the carrier, separate from the structural repair limit.

What is not typically covered: damage from a tree that was already dead, diseased, or leaning and should have been removed before the storm, since that falls under maintenance rather than a sudden loss. Document the tree's condition before removal if you can, in case the carrier asks.
