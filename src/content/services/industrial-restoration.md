A warehouse with 40,000 square feet of open floor space doesn't dry the way a house does. Water from a burst sprinkler line or a failed roof drain spreads under racking, soaks into slab joints, and sits beneath inventory pallets long after the surface looks dry. Manufacturing floors have the same problem with soot after an electrical fire: it settles into machinery housings and control panels where a standard clean won't reach it. Industrial restoration deals with scale, downtime cost, and equipment that can't just be thrown out and replaced with a trip to the hardware store.

## What Industrial Restoration actually involves

Industrial and commercial-scale losses, whether from water, fire, or storm intrusion, require different math than a residential job. The square footage is larger, the ceiling heights change how air moves, and there's usually production equipment, inventory, or racking systems in the way of a straightforward dry-out. Drying a warehouse means positioning desiccant or LGR dehumidifiers to cover cubic footage, not just room size, and running moisture meters across concrete slabs that can hold water in control joints for weeks if they're missed. On a fire loss, soot characterization matters even more in a plant setting because synthetic materials, lubricants, and chemical stock burn differently than a house fire and leave different residues on equipment surfaces. Timelines run longer than residential work, often measured in weeks rather than days, because the goal is getting the facility operational again without a callback for hidden moisture or corrosion six months later.

## Our process

1. **Facility assessment and hazard mapping.** Before any equipment goes in, we walk the affected zones with moisture meters and thermal imaging to map how far the water or smoke actually traveled, including under racking, through wall cavities, and into HVAC returns. OSHA-trained crews flag electrical and mechanical hazards before work starts.
2. **Containment and phased drying.** Rather than shutting down an entire facility, we section off affected zones so unaffected production areas can keep running. Desiccant dehumidification is sized to the actual cubic footage of the space, not guessed at.
3. **Moisture and air monitoring across the structure.** Slabs, racking bases, and wall assemblies get tracked daily with documented readings. This is also where ductwork gets checked, since contaminated air handling can spread soot or moisture into parts of the building that never saw direct damage.
4. **Equipment and inventory evaluation.** Machinery, control panels, and stock get assessed for what's salvageable with cleaning versus what needs replacement, documented for the facility's insurance adjuster and risk manager.
5. **Reconstruction and reinstallation coordination.** Once the space reads dry or clean, we coordinate with facility engineers and contractors on flooring, drywall, or equipment reinstallation so the return to production happens on a known schedule, not an open-ended one.

## What separates a good industrial restoration response from a bad one

The biggest mistake on large-loss jobs is treating square footage like a bigger version of a house job: more air movers, same approach. It doesn't work. Desiccant dehumidification sized for open warehouse volume behaves differently than refrigerant-based units, and using the wrong equipment class means a space reads dry on the surface while concrete and steel framing stay wet underneath. Another common miss is skipping HVAC and ductwork inspection after a fire or flood, which lets soot or moisture recirculate into clean areas weeks later. Adjusters reviewing industrial claims also look closely at documented moisture logs and photos tied to specific zones, not a single blanket "dried" sign-off, because business interruption coverage often hinges on proof of when each section of the facility was actually safe to reoccupy.
